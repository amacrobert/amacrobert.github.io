There used to be a fridge.

Not a metaphor. An actual fridge, in an actual kitchen, on the third floor of an office that someone leased with venture 
money it would never recoup. The fridge was stocked twice a week by a person whose entire job was stocking fridges, and
inside it you would find... God, you would find anything. Topo Chico. Eight kinds of LaCroix. Cold brew on tap next to
it, the kind with the little tap and the little chalkboard sign that said which roaster. Kombucha. Cheese. Actual 
cheese, the kind that costs money. If you wanted lunch you walked downstairs and a chef who used to work at a Michelin
star restaurant made you a bowl, and you ate it at a reclaimed-wood table next to a guy who was getting paid four
hundred grand to add a button to a settings page.

I am not making any of this up. I ate the cheese.

The thing nobody tells you about a golden age while you're in it is that it doesn't feel golden. It feels like Tuesday.
You bitch about standup dragging on. You bitch about scope creep from Product. You complain that the espresso machine
is broken again and somebody from facilities apologizes and orders a new one, a better one, on a card that nobody is
checking, because nobody is checking anything, because money is free.

That's the part. Money was free.

I don't mean cheap. I mean free. The federal interest rate was zero, or close enough that the difference was rounding.
Pension funds and sovereign wealth funds and your aunt's 401k were sloshing around looking for any yield they could
find, and the place they found it was a bunch of guys in Patagonia vests in Menlo Park who would write a check to 
anything with a deck. The checks got bigger. The valuations got crazier. The companies hired. Then they hired again. 
They paid in cash and they paid in stock and the stock kept going up because the next round was always coming, and the 
next round was always coming because money was free.

So they treated us like we were precious. Because they had to. There was a recruiter in my LinkedIn DMs every other day 
with a number that would embarrass my father. There were signing bonuses. There were sign-on RSU grants that vested into 
a down payment. There was a culture, a real culture, of "we don't do that here": we don't do crunch, we don't do on-call
without comp time, we don't do dress codes, we don't do nine to five, we don't do return-to-office because remote works
and our engineers are adults. Companies wrote blog posts about how they trusted their people. They meant it, kind of. 
Mostly they meant they couldn't afford not to mean it, because if they didn't mean it the engineer would walk across the
street and get meant-it somewhere else by Friday.

We thought it was about us. We thought we'd won some argument. We thought management had finally figured out, after a
hundred years of Taylorism and cubicles and TPS reports, that knowledge work needed a different shape, and we were the
generation lucky enough to be standing there when the lightbulb went on.

It was not about us.

In October of 2022 a guy bought Twitter. You know the story. He didn't really want to buy it; he'd made a joke offer and
the board called his bluff, and so he did the thing the joke had implied, and then he walked into the building carrying
a sink. Within a month he had fired seventy-something percent of the staff. He demanded "hardcore" commitments in 
writing, by midnight, click yes or get walked out. He killed the bird.

And here is the thing I want you to understand, the thing I think about at night. None of the other CEOs stopped him.
None of them said anything. They watched. They took notes. Some of them were on his group chat. Within a month 
Zuckerberg was calling it the year of efficiency and laying off eleven thousand people, and then ten thousand more, and
the layoffs were not a response to a downturn so much as a permission slip. Permission to do the thing they'd been
wanting to do for years. The Fed had started hiking in March. By the end of 2022 the federal funds rate was over four
percent, on its way to five and a quarter, and money was no longer free, and the calculus of how to treat an engineer 
had changed underneath us while we were still arguing about Slack emojis.

What Musk did was say it out loud: "We never thought you were precious, we thought you were expensive, and when the cost
of capital was zero the difference between precious and expensive was invisible, and now it isn't."

Everything since has been a long slow ratification of that sentence. The RTO mandates. The "low performer" memos written 
in the same language at six different companies in the same quarter, as if circulated. The stack ranks coming back under
new names. The Slack channels going quiet because half the people in them got an email at two in the morning. The 
recruiters disappearing. The grants getting smaller and the cliffs getting longer and the perks quietly thinning out. 
The snacks first, then the lunches, then the offsites, then the headcount itself.

I miss the fridge. I miss being lied to about why the fridge was there. I miss the lie because the lie was so much more
comfortable than the truth, which is that we were line items the whole time and the line was only that long because
rates were that low.

The cheese was real, though. The cheese was good.
